Before you use any prompt in this book

Read this once. It applies to every prompt in every chapter.

The one-minute version

AI writes a first draft. You are the teacher. Nothing in this book produces something that goes to a student, a family, or a permanent record without you reading it first.

Never paste these into an AI tool

  • Student names, initials or ID numbers
  • Contact details for students or families
  • Medical information, diagnoses, or protected plans
  • Confidential incident or safeguarding records
  • Any story detailed enough to identify one child

Write "a Year 5 student" or "Student A" instead.

Taking the name out is not the same as making it anonymous. A class of 28 with

one wheelchair user is identifiable without any name at all. If the detail is

what makes the request work, that is exactly the detail to leave out.

Use whatever AI tool your school has approved. If your school has not approved one, ask before putting any school information into any of them.

What AI may write, and what it may not

It may invent: teaching activities, practice questions, worked examples, fictional scenarios, sentence starters, structures and sequences.

It must never invent:

Not thisWhy
What a student said or didYou were there. It wasn't.
Research findings or quotationsIt will produce plausible fake citations
Standards, codes, or policy wordingCheck the real document
Dates, approvals, meeting recordsThese are factual records
Test scores or measured resultsThese are evidence, not text

If something you need is missing, the prompt is written to ask you rather than fill the gap. When it asks, answer — do not let it guess.

Judgements that stay with you

AI does not decide a final grade, a placement, a disciplinary outcome, or anything to do with safeguarding. It can help you draft the words. The decision is yours, and so is the responsibility.

Never let it infer a diagnosis, a home situation, a motivation, an identity, or a fixed ability level. It cannot know these, and a confident sentence about them is still a guess.

Keep any accommodation or constraint you supplied. If you said a student has extra time, that must survive into the draft.

Two habits worth building

Check the maths and the facts yourself. If you ask the model to check its own work it will say it checked. That is not verification.

Read the draft as the person receiving it. A parent email that reads fine to you at 9pm may not read fine to a family at 8am.

If a request touches harm or danger

Stop. Do not work it through with an AI tool. Follow your school's safeguarding or emergency procedure. That process exists for exactly this moment.

The difference between a worksheet that teaches and one that fills time is whether the difficulty is deliberate. These prompts build practice that ramps — starting where the class actually is, ending where you want them — and activities that keep running when you are on the other side of the room.

Chapter 3 — Assessment, Rubrics and Quizzes

40 prompts

A good question does more than sort right from wrong — it tells you *what* the student misunderstood. That is the idea running through this whole chapter: distractors that mean something, rubrics a student can act on, and checks that separate real understanding from having copied the method.

Worth knowing before you start

Check the maths and the facts yourself. This is the chapter where an unverified error reaches a student's grade. The model will produce a wrong answer key with complete confidence.

Ask what each wrong option reveals. That turns marking into diagnosis and costs you nothing extra.

Single-point rubrics — one column describing proficient — are faster to write and far faster to mark than a full grid. Several prompts here use them.

The email you have been putting off is usually the one where the facts are clear but the tone is not. These prompts separate the two: state what happened, describe what you have already tried, make one specific ask — and leave out everything you cannot actually know.

Worth knowing before you start

Describe behaviour, never character. "Three tasks are not recorded as submitted" is a fact. "He is unmotivated" is a guess about a child you are writing to their family about.

Chapter 6 — Report Card Comments

30 prompts

Twenty-eight comments in one sitting is the job nobody plans for and everybody dreads. These prompts do the drafting so you can do the judging — every comment tied to something you actually recorded, with one next step the student can act on.

What's in here

Worth knowing before you start

Use initials or "Student A". These comments go into a permanent record. The drafting should not put a child's name into an AI tool.

Give it your real evidence — scores, dates, things you wrote down. A comment built on nothing reads like a comment built on nothing.

Do not let it invent a strength to balance the structure. A manufactured compliment is the fastest way for a family to stop trusting the rest.

Vary the openings. Twenty-eight comments that all begin the same way are recognisable as generated, and parents compare.

Most behaviour problems are routine problems wearing a disguise. These prompts work on the structures — how a transition runs, what you say in the first ten seconds, how a conversation gets repaired afterwards — rather than on the student.

Worth knowing before you start

This chapter has the firmest limit in the book. If a situation involves harm, abuse, or immediate danger, stop and follow your school's safeguarding procedure. Do not work it through with an AI tool. Nothing here is a substitute for that process, and several prompts are written to say so and stop.

A restorative conversation is not a forced apology. If a draft demands one, change it — several prompts rule it out explicitly.

25 prompts

None of this is teaching, and all of it takes teaching time. These prompts clear the paperwork quickly and in a professional register — brief, specific, and asking plainly for what you need.

Worth knowing before you start

Put the ask in the first line. Everything after it is context, and half your readers will not reach it.

Do not promise a student outcome in a professional goal. "I will do X and gather evidence Y" is a goal you control; "scores will rise 10%" is not.

Anything with a date, an approval, a cost or a policy reference — check it against the real document. The model will produce a plausible date.

Ask for the one-line hallway version alongside the email. You will use it more.

The rest of the book works across subjects. This chapter does not — each prompt is built around how one subject actually behaves, and what goes wrong when a general tool is pointed at it.

What's in here

Worth knowing before you start

Verify everything factual in this chapter. Historical dates, scientific values, quotations from texts, worked calculations — this is where a confident wrong answer does the most damage, because it looks exactly like a right one.

For science, never accept invented data. Predicted patterns and measured results are different things, and these prompts keep them apart.

For social studies, ask for the contested question rather than the settled summary. That is where the thinking is.

For arts and PE, be specific about equipment and space. Generic plans assume a room you do not have.

How to run these

Keep each workflow in one conversation. Steps say things like "using the unit plan above" — that only works if the plan is above. Do not start a fresh chat between steps.

If a step asks you something, answer it. Pushing on with a gap makes every later step guess.

Stop between steps whenever you want. A workflow is a suggested route, not a contract. Several of these end by telling you what they could not do — that is the workflow working, not failing.
